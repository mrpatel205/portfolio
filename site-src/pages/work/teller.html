The legacy system supported withdrawals, deposits and payments across the bank’s 437 branches, used daily by more than 1,000 frontline bankers.

A significant withdrawal could mean switching between up to eight systems to check thresholds, authorise cash and work out what came next. Tellers memorised shortcodes and policy rules to keep work moving.

That knowledge made the legacy system dependable for experienced bankers, but hard to learn and costly to maintain. Replacing it risked moving the complexity onto tellers.

The programme moved withdrawal, deposit and payment transactions into the colleague banking channel. The design challenge was moving the work, its decision logic and its links to branch hardware with it.

Withdrawals: I joined after the core interaction model was set. I owned cash breakup, cash dispense and the transaction summary, moved the design onto the bank’s new design system, and completed the engineering handover.

Deposits: I led the work from discovery through handover, covering the core flow plus cheque, third-party and business deposits, including rollback and edit scenarios.

The programme was planned as a big-bang release across withdrawals, deposits and payments. We designed one transaction type at a time, treating each as a connected workflow and scaling the process to its complexity and available capacity.

Where time allowed, I observed teller work in branches, ran moderated tests and held SME workshops. That surfaced keyboard-first habits and cash-recycler dependencies.

When time was tight, I focused on the riskiest decisions and worked with product and engineering as policy and API readiness changed.

Five days in branches and 82 observed transactions gave Deposits real-world context. Moderated rounds and surveys then helped validate high-risk interactions before handover.

Six connected challenges shaped the work: reducing system switching, uncovering what branch practice revealed beyond the requirements, designing around hidden device dependencies, changing habits carried over from the legacy system, extending a customer-focused design system for colleague work, and aligning decisions across a broad team. Together, they pointed to one goal: make teller work simpler without losing the safeguards it relied on.

Systems thinking User and wireflows A significant cash withdrawal could mean switching between up to 8 systems. Tellers held the order and the policy rules in their heads. That worked for veterans, but not for new tellers without legacy-system muscle memory.

What I did

I drove the early decision between a full-page, side-sheet and Hub approach, then shaped the Hub as a dynamic task list for withdrawals and payments. It consolidated steps, showed only the tasks relevant to each transaction, and carried policy checks into the flow. Deposits were different: observation showed about 80% were simple, so we left the Hub out to avoid adding steps.

 Contextual enquiry Survey creation and synthesis Withdrawal observation happened in fragments: half-days squeezed between other work, under pressure to deliver high-fidelity screens. Deposits carried far more machine interaction, so designing it without real branch context was too big a risk.

What I did

For Deposits, I arranged a four-day immersion across four branches before design, using observation to check the brief against teller practice. I found two missing requirements: the TCR’s inventory warning colour and a way to roll back cash already fed during a count. I brought both into the design, making machine feedback and recovery part of the transaction flow.

 Systems thinking Contextual enquiry The legacy system read and wrote live state to the TCR and card terminals, which made Deposits far more complex than Withdrawals. Tellers also ran it on fixed 24-inch monitors, often across several resized windows the brief never mentioned.

What I did

I designed the Deposits flows end to end for simple, mixed, third-party and cheque acceptance, including device and API states that could interrupt a transaction: an occupied TCR, unavailable inventory, no cash, or a transaction already in progress. For fixed 24-inch teller screens, I reorganised the TCR panel for narrow windows instead of simply shrinking it. The narrow-window layout still needs validation.

 Usability testing Contextual enquiry Tellers read the new system through a legacy lens, expecting the teller cash recycler (TCR) to balance itself as it always had. Neither TCR screen had an existing pattern to start from, and with cash there was no margin for error.

What I did

To keep legacy TCR assumptions from carrying into the new colleague banking channel, I separated standard accounting inputs from device-specific controls and tested the riskiest interactions first. For withdrawals, I added an Automatic/Custom choice so denomination fields appeared only when needed. For deposits, I combined the count into one live screen and added Roll back to recount. In testing, “Amounts match” and “Counted: $X” gave tellers reassurance.

 Design systems contribution Visual design The new system’s design system was built for customer apps, with few shared patterns for colleague-facing work. I was also new to Figma and the bank’s atomic design conventions, learning the tool and the system’s logic at the same time.

What I did

I learned Figma and the bank’s atomic design conventions while adapting a customer-focused library for colleague work. I contributed reusable patterns, including a horizontal radio group and a progress tracker built with Business Lending. Through the Design Guild, I shared colleague use cases and helped extend the library into colleague journeys; other teams adopted some of the patterns.

 Workshop facilitation Usability testing Teller spanned several designers, product, SMEs, BAs and an offshore team working on Deposits. Shared patterns, like the Hub’s cards and error states, needed agreement across all of them, not just sign-off at the end.

What I did

I set up quick huddles with Product, SMEs and BAs to resolve requirement questions, and ran weekly tests to validate flows. I iterated the Hub card in Elevate design sessions, facilitated workshops on error states, and ran cross-designer flow reviews so shared patterns stayed consistent. I also supported the lead researcher with an end-to-end withdrawal test in a mock branch lab.
