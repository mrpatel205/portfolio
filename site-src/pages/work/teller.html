At National Australia Bank, eBOBS had powered branch teller transactions — withdrawals, deposits, payments — for nearly 40 years across all 437 of NAB's branches in Australia, run daily by an estimated 1,000+ frontline bankers.

Many transactions meant switching systems: checking policy thresholds information, and entering one of many of 4-digit numeric codes tellers had to memorise to initiate a transaction.

It was reliable and earned genuine loyalty once tellers learned it. But it came with a high barrier to entry. For the business it was too costly to maintain, with too few engineers knowing how to build on the legacy code.

Over three years, the programme brought together a cross-functional team of 6 designers and 3 agile squads, working closely with the SME partner Future of Retail, toward a shared vision: removing all switching of systems by migrating withdrawal, deposit, and payment transactions into nabONE — the colleague banking channel built on a modern, API-enabled web stack and NAB's new design system.

I picked the Withdrawals epic half way through the programme, after the previous design team had nailed down the core interaction model along with initial designs in Nab's old design system. My job was to own the key complexity in the base experience, uplift everything to the new design system, and take it all the way through to a complete, responsive handover for engineering.

Deposits had no head start — I owned it from the design brief through to shipped, responsive design, across every sub-transaction type it spawned.

The programme was planned as one big-bang release covering every withdrawal, payment and deposit transaction type, but we designed them one at a time. Each ran its own design process, sized to its breadth and complexity, and to the time and capacity we actually had.

Some got deep discovery: time in branch, moderated testing and workshops with our SMEs. Others didn't have that luxury, so the process flexed to match.

Without real-world feedback for ~18 months, we used testing rounds and branch observation to cut experience risk before go-live.

This wasn't a blank canvas — the data, workflows, and trust already existed, built over years with the system it replaced, and the real test was earning that confidence back. That shaped everything: a dependency that shifted mid-project, practices that needed questioning, and work split between a wider team and solo ownership. Open any challenge below.

Usability testing Contextual enquiry Tellers read nabONE through an eBOBS lens, expecting the teller cash recycler (TCR) to balance itself as it always had. Neither TCR screen had an existing pattern to start from, and with cash there was no margin for error.

What I did

I split the TCR into standard accounting inputs and TCR-specific patterns, and tested the TCR-specific ones hardest, since they carried the most risk of old habits misleading tellers. Withdrawal got an Automatic/Custom toggle that hides break-up fields until needed. Deposit's two-screen count became one live screen with Roll back to recount. “Amounts match” and “Counted: $X” tested well as reassurance.

 Systems thinking User and wireflows A significant cash withdrawal could mean switching between up to 8 systems. Tellers held the order and the policy rules in their heads. That worked for veterans, but not for new tellers without eBOBS muscle memory.

What I did

I drove the early call between a full page and a side sheet, then shaped the Hub: a dynamic task list for withdrawals and payments that consolidates steps and shows only what a transaction needs, with conditional logic across flows. The system now carries policy tellers used to memorise. Deposits skipped the Hub on purpose: 80% of deposit tasks were already quick.

 Design systems contribution Visual design nabONE's design system was built for customer apps, with few shared patterns for colleague-facing work. I was also new to Figma and NAB's atomic design conventions, learning the tool and the system's logic at the same time.

What I did

I built fluency fast, then started giving back. I contributed components to the shared library for colleague use cases, including a horizontal radio group and a progress tracker built with the Business Lending team, plus patterns other teams adopted. Through the nabONE Design Guild, I helped extend the system from customer journeys into colleague ones.

 Contextual enquiry Survey creation and synthesis Withdrawal observation happened in fragments: half-days squeezed between other work, under pressure to deliver high-fidelity screens. Deposits carried far more machine interaction, so designing it without real branch context was too big a risk.

What I did

For Deposits, I built in a four-day immersion across four branches before designing anything, checking the requirements against what tellers actually did. It surfaced two gaps no document had captured: a TCR inventory warning colour, and a way to roll back cash already fed in mid-count. Both became part of the Deposits design.

 Systems thinking Contextual enquiry eBOBS read and wrote live state to the TCR and card terminals, which made Deposits far more complex than Withdrawals. Tellers also ran it on fixed 24" monitors, often across several resized windows the brief never mentioned.

What I did

I designed every Deposits flow end to end, including simple, mixed, third-party and cheque acceptance, and the device states around them: TCR in use, inventory API down, no notes in the TCR, transaction already in progress. For narrow windows, I rebuilt the TCR panel's order rather than scaling it down. That layout is still untested at narrow widths.

 Workshop facilitation Usability testing Teller spanned several designers, product, SMEs, BAs and an offshore team working on Deposits. Shared patterns, like the Hub's cards and error states, needed agreement across all of them, not just sign-off at the end.

What I did

I set up chat huddles with Product, SMEs and BAs to settle requirement questions quickly, and ran weekly testing for fast validation. We iterated the Hub card through Elevate design sessions, ran team workshops on error states and had flows tested across designers. I also helped the lead researcher run an end-to-end withdrawal test in a mock branch lab.

 Bankers picked it up quickly in the first branches. Bankers like the overall flow.
