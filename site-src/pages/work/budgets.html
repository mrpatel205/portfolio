The major bank’s FY24 research showed that budgeting mattered to some customers, particularly those aged 18–35. In discovery, customers told us they tracked their money in spreadsheets and “bucket” accounts, sometimes across multiple banks. They couldn’t compare category spending with a budget in the app. That gave us a clear opportunity: bring this kind of tracking into the app.

I was the sole designer and researcher on Budgets, working with the product owner, business analyst, engineering and accessibility teams. I planned and ran the research, turned what we learned into design decisions, and prepared the final designs for handover.

I used two rounds of research and testing to define and refine the design.

First, customer interviews and concept testing helped us choose category budgets for the MVP. Then I tested the prototype with customers and used what I learned to refine the budget overview, progress indicators and category selection before handover.

The MVP was initially scoped around account budgets, while the team was also exploring category budgets. We also needed to rank ten more concepts for FY26 planning, so I set up research to compare the two models and help the team decide what to take forward. I spoke with 14 participants. The results showed no clear pattern across customer groups, so I used them as a guide.

Concept testing didn’t show a clear preference, and we found no reliable pattern across customer groups. A delivery review showed category budgets could use existing categories, while account budgets depended on separate work. At the steering committee, the team chose category budgets based on the research and what they could deliver.

How might Budgets offer useful support without assuming it could solve broader financial hardship?

I spoke with several financial counsellors; one highlighted the practical advice couples who struggle to budget often need. For Release 1, we added a link to the bank’s budgeting advice page. It wasn’t the target solution, but helped meet the design’s inclusion requirements while leaving room for more tailored support in future releases.

Budgets sat within the Spending mini app and used its category colours. In a prototype test, 10 of 12 customers couldn’t quickly tell which categories were over budget. We needed to make budget status easier to spot while keeping those category colours.

Green for met and red for over budget worked in testing. Spending already used its own category colours, and another team was considering a wider Categories update. Changing the full palette was outside this feature’s scope, so I looked for ways to make budget status clearer within the existing system.

Colour alone still made people scan every row to find what needed attention. I added clear “over” and “left” amounts, then sorted over-budget categories with the biggest overspend first.

As my work on Budgets came to a close, the diary study I’d formulated was carried out with colleagues testing the beta. It gathered 10 first-use responses and 22 weekly follow-ups. People found setup easy and suggested clearer navigation, more category transaction detail and budget alerts, giving the team feedback to consider for future improvements.

In August 2025, around 4,000 customers logged in to Budgets. They opened it an average of 1.7 times that month and averaged 5.88 sessions across the app overall. These figures show customers used Budgets, but don’t tell us whether it changed how they managed their budgets.

Research works best aimed at what only customers can answer. Next time, I’d settle feasibility first with a delivery spike, rather than waiting on a concept test to choose between Accounts and Categories. And I’d screen on recent behaviour, not self-report: participants who already budgeted well cost sessions and weakened the evidence.
