Modernising the teller experience

Company
Major bank
Industry
Fin-Tech
Duration
Oct 24 to Jun 26
Role
Senior Product Designer

Situation

A 40-year system powered branch work

The legacy system supported withdrawals, deposits and payments across the bank’s 437 branches, used daily by more than 1,000 frontline bankers.

Situation

Tellers held the missing instructions

A significant withdrawal could mean switching between up to eight systems to check thresholds, authorise cash and work out what came next. Tellers memorised shortcodes and policy rules to keep work moving.

That knowledge made the legacy system dependable for experienced bankers, but hard to learn and costly to maintain. Replacing it risked moving the complexity onto tellers.

Situation

Rebuilding the colleague banking channel

The programme moved withdrawal, deposit and payment transactions into the colleague banking channel. The design challenge was moving the work, its decision logic and its links to branch hardware with it.

Task

Withdrawals & Deposits

Withdrawals: I joined after the core interaction model was set. I owned cash breakup, cash dispense and the transaction summary, moved the design onto the bank’s new design system, and completed the engineering handover.

Deposits: I led the work from discovery through handover, covering the core flow plus cheque, third-party and business deposits, including rollback and edit scenarios.

Skills used

  • Systems Thinking
  • Design Systems Contribution
  • Contextual Enquiry

  • Rapid AI Prototyping

Approach

Rigour set upfront, flexed by time

The programme planned one release across withdrawals, deposits and payments, and each transaction type was rated against the bank’s design-quality framework. Its must-win status lifted withdrawals and deposits from Silver to Gold, which set the minimum design activities.

Under pressure, I moved to mid-fi with fewer test rounds but kept the Gold bar, and focused testing on the riskiest steps: cash break-up and dispense.

When time allowed, branch observation, moderated testing and SME workshops shaped the work.

Challenges

Learning the tellers' language first

Tellers used terms I did not know: cash recyclers, authorisations, policy thresholds. Requirements alone would have put my assumptions between them and their work. So I mapped their vocabulary: each circle is something tellers handle, each arrow a rule, like ‘a power of attorney can replace an authority’. That let me design flows where each screen showed what tellers expected next.

Designing for tellers meant solving problems that never showed on a screen: a domain I did not know, a design system with few patterns for fast, complex colleague work, an API that did not arrive and six designers on one journey. Each one put the teller's experience at risk, so each one changed how I worked.

Research

Validate the risky steps

Five days in branches and 82 observed transactions gave Deposits real-world context. Moderated rounds and surveys then helped validate high-risk interactions before handover.

Research

Principles that guided every flow

The design principles set by the previous team were born out of the need to speed up bankers’ time, reduce errors and remove the cognitive load. There were more than these, although these are the ones that guided our design thinking for interaction design and content.

  • System-guided

    Context arrives when it's needed.

  • Obvious

    Dynamic fields. Clear confirmations.

  • Flexible

    Move back and forth. Inputs stay.

  • Simplified

    No codes or shortcuts to start.

Solution

One journey, many hands

A teller moves through one journey, but it was designed by many hands. Withdrawals was the most complex transaction, with the most steps, and six designers shaped it. Deposits I owned end to end. The moments below are a small slice of a release that ran to hundreds of screens, chosen to show where my work shaped what tellers see.

Solution

Know what needs to happen next

Complex withdrawals needed more than a sequence of screens. I developed the inherited Hub design into a task list that showed what mattered for the transaction, working with the design-system team on its format. Tellers could see what was complete and step back to edit.

  • System-guided
  • Flexible

Solution

Keep everyday deposits quick

Branch observation showed that most deposits were simple and completed in under two minutes in the legacy system. We left the Hub out of Deposits. Applying the same pattern everywhere would have added a step where tellers did not need it.

  • Flexible

Solution

Live status against the total

The running total updates as tellers type. Orange turns green once every input adds up. One of the small things that keeps tellers fast: they see the colour change in their periphery, without looking at the total.

  • Obvious

Impact

About 3 in 4 in-scope transactions now run in the new system

Reflections

What I’d do differently

See other epics’ requirements sooner. I couldn’t see them, so shared patterns diverged. I’d push for visibility early, so patterns align before release.

Don’t bank on an external API. I’d explore a fallback with the PM early, rather than absorb about five sprints of rework.

Do more field research up front. Seeing tellers work earlier, in full days, would change my planning and bring Withdrawals’ first design closer.
